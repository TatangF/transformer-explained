A language model reads a sequence of tokens and predicts the next one.
The prediction is a probability distribution over the whole vocabulary.
The model picks a token, adds it to the input, and predicts again.
This loop is called autoregressive generation.

Attention lets every token look at the other tokens in the context window.
Each token builds a query, a key and a value from its own vector.
The query of one token is compared with the keys of all the other tokens.
The scores become weights, and the weights mix the values into a new vector.
A token that matters a lot for the current word receives a large weight.
A token that does not matter receives a weight close to zero.

Older recurrent networks read one word after the other.
They could not use the whole graphics card at the same time during training.
Information from the start of a long sentence faded away before the end.
The transformer connects every token to every other token in a single step.
All the tokens are processed at once, so the computation is a big matrix product.

Several heads run in parallel, and each head can look for a different relation.
One head may link a verb to its subject, another may link a pronoun to its noun.
The outputs of the heads are joined together and mixed by a linear layer.

The context window has a fixed size.
A token outside the window is invisible to the model.
Inside the window, every pair of tokens produces one score.
For a window of N tokens there are N times N scores.
If the window becomes twice as long, the grid of scores becomes four times bigger.
If the window becomes ten times longer, the grid becomes one hundred times bigger.
This is why a very long context is expensive in time and in memory.

The full pipeline is short.
First, the text becomes tokens and the tokens become vectors with a position.
Then attention exchanges information between the tokens.
Then a small dense network refines each token on its own.
These two steps are repeated in every layer.
At the end, a softmax turns the final scores into probabilities.
The most likely token, or a sampled one, is the next token of the text.
